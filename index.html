Autism Spectrum Disorder in Children:
Diagnostics and Therapy

A structured review of the current state of diagnostics and therapy for children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), followed by a critical examination of promising biological hypotheses — gut microbiota, increased intestinal permeability and aldehydes as potential neurotoxic factors.

Read the outline Research hypotheses

Not medical advice. This page is a scientific and educational overview only. It is not a recommendation for diagnosis or treatment. Established methods and experimental hypotheses are kept clearly separate.

Why this matters

Autism spectrum disorder is a heterogeneous neurodevelopmental condition characterised by differences in social communication and by restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour and interests. Reported prevalence has risen substantially over recent decades, and early identification is widely regarded as the key to timely, individualised support.

~1 in 31

children aged 8 years identified with ASD in the most recent U.S. ADDM Network surveillance report

Early

screening and diagnosis are recommended for better access to developmental and behavioural support

Heterogeneous

aetiology and presentation — no single cause, biomarker or treatment fits all children

Prevalence figures vary by country, surveillance method and diagnostic criteria. The numbers and sources on this page are being finalised for publication.

Research hypotheses

Section 4 of the synopsis reviews three biological directions. Each is labelled by the strength of current evidence — none of them is an established treatment target today.

4.1 Emerging evidence

Gut microbiota

The gut microbiome interacts with immune, metabolic and nervous systems through the so-called gut–brain axis. Studies in children with ASD report differences in microbial composition, and animal work shows that microbiota can influence behaviour. Causality in humans remains unproven, and diet, medication and other confounders complicate interpretation.

Association, not causation.

4.2 Preliminary

Increased intestinal permeability

The intestinal barrier regulates what passes from the gut lumen into the body. Increased permeability (“leaky gut”) has been linked to inflammatory and metabolic processes and reported in some ASD cohorts, but cohorts are small, assays differ and results are inconsistent.

Limited and mixed evidence.

4.3 Hypothesis

Aldehydes as neurotoxic factors

Aldehydes are reactive compounds formed endogenously and encountered in the environment; they are detoxified by aldehyde dehydrogenases. Disturbed aldehyde metabolism is biologically plausible as a contributor to neurodevelopmental differences, but direct evidence in ASD is scarce and largely preclinical. This part is presented explicitly as a hypothesis.

Hypothesis — under investigation.

Evidence levels used here: Emerging evidence Preliminary Hypothesis

Aldehydes in the body — the established part

The hypothesis in 4.3 concerns a real and well-studied family of molecules. What mainstream biochemistry actually supports:

  • Formaldehyde is a normal endogenous one-carbon metabolite, generated during methyl-group metabolism and from methanol and demethylation reactions.
  • It is short-lived: it is cleared mainly through glutathione-dependent pathways and aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH), being oxidised to formate and fed into one-carbon and folate pools.
  • At elevated concentrations, aldehydes cross-link proteins and DNA and are neurotoxic; impaired clearance is associated with oxidative stress and inflammation.
  • In autism spectrum disorder specifically, a causal role is not established — the evidence is preliminary and largely preclinical, which is why the topic appears here as a hypothesis and not as a treatment target.

What is supported — and what is not

Keeping this line clear is a central goal of the synopsis.

Supported by current evidence

  • Systematic developmental screening and early comprehensive assessment.
  • Behavioural and developmental interventions (e.g. naturalistic developmental-behavioural approaches, parent-mediated programmes).
  • Speech and language therapy and occupational therapy as indicated.
  • Educational support tailored to the child.
  • Selective medication for co-occurring symptoms (e.g. irritability), prescribed and monitored by clinicians.
  • Support and training for families and caregivers.

Not supported / not recommended as ASD treatment

  • Chelation therapy.
  • Hyperbaric oxygen therapy.
  • Secretin.
  • Antibiotics as a treatment for ASD itself.
  • Restrictive elimination diets marketed as ASD treatment.
  • Any intervention that has not been shown effective in controlled studies.

Some of these carry real risk of harm. Decisions about care belong with qualified clinicians.
