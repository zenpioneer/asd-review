Diagnostics and therapy of autism spectrum disorder in children

A structured review of current diagnostics and therapy worldwide, followed by a critical look at three biological hypotheses — the gut microbiota, increased intestinal permeability, and aldehydes as potential neurotoxic factors.

Key points

  • ASD is heterogeneous — no single cause, biomarker or treatment fits every child.
  • Early identification opens earlier access to developmental and behavioural support.
  • Evidence-based care rests on behavioural and developmental therapy, speech and occupational therapy, educational support and family involvement; medication targets specific co-occurring symptoms.
  • The biological hypotheses reviewed here — microbiota, intestinal permeability, aldehydes — are not established treatment targets.

This is a scientific and educational overview, not medical advice. Established methods and experimental hypotheses are kept strictly apart.

01

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition defined by persistent differences in social communication and social interaction, together with restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities. Its defining feature is breadth: the same diagnosis spans children who do not speak and children with fluent language, children with intellectual disability and children of average or above-average ability, with needs that change over the lifespan.

Terminology and diagnosis

The modern category took shape when DSM-5 (2013) replaced the separate diagnoses of autistic disorder, Asperger’s disorder and pervasive developmental disorder not otherwise specified with a single, dimensional label; DSM-5-TR (2022) revised the text without changing the core criteria. ICD-11 (2019/2022) uses a compatible approach under code 6A02, with specifiers for accompanying intellectual disability and functional language impairment. Both systems require that features be present in early development and cause clinically significant impairment, although they may only become apparent later, when social demands exceed capacity.

Relevance and prevalence

Reported prevalence has risen substantially over recent decades. In the most recent U.S. surveillance summary, about 1 in 31 children aged 8 years met the criteria for ASD, and estimates from other countries range from well below 1% to around 2–3%, depending on how cases are counted. The increase is generally attributed to a combination of broader diagnostic criteria, greater awareness, diagnostic substitution, earlier detection and better access to services, rather than to any single cause; the relative weight of these factors is still debated.

ASD is multifactorial. Twin and family studies indicate a strong genetic contribution, with a substantial number of rare and common variants involved, but no single gene or environmental exposure accounts for more than a small fraction of cases. Most children have no identifiable syndromic cause, and the search for clinically useful biomarkers continues.

Why early identification matters

Signs of ASD can often be observed in the second year of life, and reliable diagnosis is possible by around 18–24 months in experienced hands. Early identification matters because it opens access to developmental and behavioural support during a period of rapid brain development, reduces the time families spend seeking answers, and allows co-occurring conditions to be recognised. The evidence that earlier intervention alters long-term outcome is encouraging but not uniform; the realistic goal is earlier access to support and improved quality of life, not a “cure”.

Scope and method of this synopsis

This synopsis summarises the current state of diagnostics and therapy worldwide and then examines three biological hypotheses that are actively discussed in the literature. Established practice is kept separate from experimental hypotheses throughout: none of the hypotheses reviewed is a validated diagnostic or therapeutic method, and nothing here is a treatment recommendation.

02

Diagnostics — current state worldwide

There is no laboratory test for ASD. Diagnosis rests on a structured clinical assessment of development and behaviour, informed by the caregiver’s history and by observations across settings.

Diagnostic criteria

DSM-5 and DSM-5-TR define ASD along two domains — persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction, and restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities — with severity specified for each. ICD-11 (6A02) mirrors this structure and adds explicit qualifiers for intellectual development and language. Because the criteria are behavioural, the same presentation can be described with either system, and cross-walking between them is generally straightforward.

Screening and early detection

Developmental surveillance is recommended at every well-child visit, with standardised screening at around 18 and 24 months. Validated parent-report tools such as the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised with Follow-up (M-CHAT-R/F) flag children who need further assessment; a positive screen is not a diagnosis. Early behavioural signs include reduced response to name, limited joint attention, few gestures such as pointing, delayed or atypical language, and loss of previously acquired skills.

Clinical assessment

Best practice is multidisciplinary and family-centred. Standardised instruments are used alongside clinical judgement: observational measures such as ADOS-2 and caregiver interviews such as ADI-R are common reference tools, though they require training and are not mandatory in every setting. Assessment usually also covers cognitive and adaptive functioning (for example Bayley, Mullen or the Vineland scales), speech and language, and motor and sensory function. Hearing must be tested, and a medical review seeks co-occurring conditions — epilepsy, sleep and gastrointestinal problems, feeding difficulties and genetic syndromes.

Medical and genetic work-up

Genetic testing is recommended as part of the evaluation, typically chromosomal microarray and fragile X testing, with exome or genome sequencing in selected cases; a yield of roughly 10–30% depending on the test and population. Metabolic testing and electroencephalography are reserved for children with specific indications such as regression, seizures or other neurological signs.

Differential diagnosis

Conditions that can resemble or accompany ASD include hearing impairment, global developmental delay and intellectual disability, developmental language disorder, social (pragmatic) communication disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, anxiety and obsessive–compulsive disorder, and rare syndromes such as Rett syndrome. Overlap is the rule rather than the exception, so assessment often identifies several co-occurring needs rather than a single exclusion.

Access, inequities and limitations

Availability of timely assessment varies widely between and within countries. Waiting times of a year or more are common, and access is uneven by region, income and language. Girls and some cultural groups are diagnosed later or less often, in part because presentations differ from the classic picture. Instrument administration depends on trained clinicians, which limits capacity. This variability — in criteria, tools and services — is the main reason prevalence figures and diagnostic practice differ so much across the world.

03

Therapy — current state worldwide

There is no cure for ASD, and no intervention works for every child. The goals of care are functional communication, adaptive skills, participation in everyday life, quality of life, and the reduction of distress — tailored to the individual and to the family.

Behavioural and developmental interventions

The best-studied approaches are behavioural and developmental. Early intensive behavioural intervention, including programmes based on applied behaviour analysis (ABA), targets skills in small structured steps. Naturalistic developmental behavioural interventions (NDBI) — such as the Early Start Denver Model, JASPER and pivotal response treatment — embed learning in play and everyday interaction, and parent-mediated approaches train caregivers to deliver the core techniques. Randomised trials and meta-analyses show moderate, but heterogeneous, benefits for language, cognitive and adaptive outcomes; effect sizes vary with intensity, age at start and the outcome measured, and study quality is uneven.

Communication, motor and sensory support

Speech and language therapy addresses communication at every level, from pre-verbal skills to conversation, and may use augmentative and alternative communication such as picture-exchange systems (PECS) or speech-generating devices. Occupational therapy targets daily living, play, motor skills and sensory processing; sensory-integration therapy is widely used but has weaker and mixed evidence. Physical therapy is added when motor difficulties interfere with function.

Education

Educational provision ranges from mainstream settings with support to specialised programmes. Structured teaching approaches such as TEACCH, visual supports and individualised education plans are widely used; inclusive education with appropriate support is the goal in many systems. The quality and availability of educational support often has more day-to-day impact on the child and family than any clinic-based intervention.

Medication for co-occurring symptoms

No medication treats the core features of ASD. Two antipsychotics, risperidone and aripiprazole, are approved in several countries for irritability and aggression in children and adolescents, with monitoring for weight gain, metabolic and movement side effects. Methylphenidate has moderate evidence for co-occurring attention problems; selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors are used for anxiety and obsessive–compulsive symptoms with mixed results; melatonin can help sleep onset. Medication is best understood as an adjunct for specific symptoms, prescribed and monitored by clinicians.

Family support

Parent training, psychoeducation, respite and peer support reduce caregiver stress and improve the fit between the child’s needs and the environment. Because families deliver most day-to-day support, their wellbeing is a legitimate and important target of care.

Supported by current evidence

  • Systematic developmental screening and early comprehensive assessment.
  • Behavioural and developmental interventions — naturalistic developmental-behavioural approaches, parent-mediated programmes.
  • Speech and language therapy and occupational therapy as indicated.
  • Educational support tailored to the child.
  • Selective medication for specific co-occurring symptoms, prescribed and monitored by clinicians.
  • Support and training for families and caregivers.

Not supported as ASD treatment

  • Chelation therapy.
  • Hyperbaric oxygen therapy.
  • Secretin.
  • Antibiotics as a treatment for ASD itself.
  • Restrictive elimination diets marketed as ASD treatment.
  • Any intervention not shown effective in controlled studies.

Some of these carry a real risk of harm. Decisions about care belong with qualified clinicians.

Evidence quality across this field is uneven: sample sizes are often small, outcome measures differ between studies, and blinding in behavioural trials is difficult. Claims of large benefits should be read with that in mind.

04

Biological hypotheses and research directions

Alongside established clinical practice, three biological directions receive sustained research attention. All are genuine scientific questions; none is an established diagnostic marker or treatment target. Each carries a label for the strength of current evidence.

4.1 Emerging

Gut microbiota

The gut microbiome interacts with immune, metabolic and nervous systems through the so-called gut–brain axis, signalling via the vagus nerve, immune mediators and microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids. Studies in children with ASD report differences in microbial composition and in metabolite profiles, and animal work shows that transferring microbiota can influence behaviour in mice. However, children with ASD often have selective diets, constipation and frequent antibiotic exposure, which themselves change the microbiome; careful analyses suggest that diet may explain much of the association. Causality in humans remains unproven, and probiotics are not an established treatment.

Association, not causation.

4.2 Preliminary

Increased intestinal permeability

The intestinal barrier, sealed by tight junctions between epithelial cells, regulates what passes from the gut lumen into the body. Increased permeability — often called “leaky gut” — has been reported in subsets of children with ASD and linked to inflammatory and metabolic processes. Results are, however, inconsistent: cohorts are small, assays and permeability markers differ between studies, and many children have gastrointestinal symptoms for reasons unrelated to ASD. There is no validated permeability test for clinical use, and treatments aimed at the barrier remain experimental.

Limited and mixed evidence.

4.3 Hypothesis

Aldehydes as neurotoxic factors

Aldehydes are reactive compounds formed endogenously and encountered in the environment; they are detoxified by alcohol and aldehyde dehydrogenases and by glutathione. Disturbed aldehyde metabolism is biologically plausible as a contributor to neurodevelopmental differences — for example through oxidative stress, protein and DNA cross-linking, or altered one-carbon metabolism — but direct evidence in ASD is scarce and largely preclinical. This part is presented explicitly as a hypothesis that requires rigorous testing, not as a treatment rationale.

Hypothesis — under investigation.

Background

Aldehydes in the body — the established part

The hypothesis in 4.3 concerns a real and well-studied family of molecules. What mainstream biochemistry actually supports:

  • Formaldehyde is a normal endogenous one-carbon metabolite, generated during methyl-group metabolism and from methanol and demethylation reactions.
  • It is short-lived: it is cleared mainly through glutathione-dependent pathways and aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH), oxidised to formate and fed into one-carbon and folate pools.
  • At elevated concentrations, aldehydes cross-link proteins and DNA and are neurotoxic; impaired clearance is associated with oxidative stress and inflammation.
  • In autism spectrum disorder specifically, a causal role is not established — the evidence is preliminary and largely preclinical, which is why the topic is presented as a hypothesis and not as a treatment target.

One related line of research concerns folate and one-carbon metabolism: cerebral folate deficiency has been described in a small subgroup of children with ASD, and folinic acid (leucovorin) has been studied as a possible treatment in selected cases. This remains an area of active, and contested, investigation rather than established practice.

05

Conclusion

Diagnostics and therapy for ASD rest on solid, if imperfect, ground; the biology is where the uncertainty lies — and where rigorous research is most needed.

What is established: ASD is diagnosed clinically, through multidisciplinary assessment using developmental history, structured observation and standardised tools, supported by medical and genetic evaluation where indicated. Early identification is recommended because it opens access to support. Effective care combines behavioural and developmental interventions, speech and language therapy, occupational therapy, educational provision and family support, with medication reserved for specific co-occurring symptoms. Several widely marketed “treatments” are unsupported and some are harmful.

What is not established: the three hypotheses reviewed here — the gut microbiota, increased intestinal permeability and aldehyde metabolism — are plausible, actively studied and, so far, unproven as causes or treatment targets in ASD. They belong to research, not to clinical practice, and should not be presented to families as validated options.

Promising directions include larger longitudinal cohorts, the search for reproducible biomarkers and biological subgroups, well-powered randomised trials of interventions, and mechanistic studies of gut–brain signalling and one-carbon metabolism. Progress will depend as much on careful, transparent methods and on separating hypothesis from evidence, as on any single discovery.
